# The read-aloud pass

Baseline mechanics for anything a person will say out loud. This file is plain craft, attributed
to no teacher. The lessons that came from a named source live in `lessons.md` and `delivery.md`.

Run this pass on the spoken text only, after the content is settled. It does not decide what to
say. It makes what is said survivable in the mouth and clear in the ear.

## The test that beats every rule below

Read the text out loud, at speaking pace, once. Anywhere the tongue trips, the breath runs out,
or the meaning arrives a beat late, the line is not done. Rule 16 of the WDJM Script Writing
Standards says the same thing; this file is what to look for when the read-aloud fails.

## Sentences

- One idea per sentence. If a sentence carries two, split it. The ear cannot rewind.
- One breath per sentence. Roughly twenty spoken words is the ceiling for a full sentence, and
  most sentences should be well under it. Short ones land. Long ones blur.
- Vary the length on purpose. A run of short sentences sounds like a list. A run of long ones
  sounds like a lecture. Short punch, then a longer thought, then a short punch.
- Put the important word at the end of the sentence, where the voice naturally lands.
- Prefer the order the listener needs: situation first, then the turn, then what it means.

## Words

- Contractions, always. Written English says "it is not"; spoken English says "it isn't".
- Simple words. The listener has no dictionary and no second chance.
- Say the number the way it is spoken and write it that way for the teleprompter when the
  digits are ambiguous ("twenty twenty-four", "a half", "six thousand"). Plain digits are fine
  when they read the same way they are said ("$6,000").
- Spell out anything the eye reads and the mouth cannot: "and/or", "e.g.", "etc.", "vs".
- No word the speaker would never use in conversation. If Joe would not say it across a
  table, cut it.

## Punctuation the ear cannot hear

- No parentheses, no brackets, no semicolons, no slashes. If the aside matters, make it a
  sentence. If it does not, cut it.
- No em dashes or en dashes anywhere (Joe's law, see `joe-copy-standards`). The spoken fix is
  usually a full stop.
- Colons are allowed in the teleprompter text only where the voice actually pauses and sets up
  what follows.
- No markdown inside the spoken block. No bold, no headings, no bullets. A teleprompter shows
  every character.

## Repetition and signposts

- Repetition is a tool for the ear, not a flaw. Repeat the key phrase, in the same words, when
  the listener needs to hold onto it. Do not repeat by accident.
- Signposts are spoken, not formatted. "Here's the part that matters." "Two things." "So."
  These do the job that headings do on a page.
- Past roughly thirty five seconds of speech, put in a rehook so the listener has a reason to
  stay. The `wdjm-script-writer` skill carries the rehook rule and examples; use them.

## The listener

- Speak to one person, never to a room. "You", not "you all", "everyone", or "guys".
- Name the listener's situation before naming the lesson. Relatability first, then the turn.
- Give the listener something to repeat back. If they could not tell a friend the point in one
  sentence, the point is not clear yet.

## Delivery notes stay out of the spoken block

Pauses, emphasis, vocal mode and gesture belong in a separate delivery notes block after the
spoken text. Never inline. A teleprompter that shows "[pause]" makes the speaker say "pause".
