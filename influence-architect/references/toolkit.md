# Toolkit

The questions, the reframe patterns, the frames, the objection protocol, the money moments, the metaphors and the delivery notes. Each tool carries its mechanism, a sales example, and its general form. Attribution: lines in quotation marks that are Eli's come from his Objection Mastery playbook, his Twelve Frames reference or his doctrine file. Lines marked (constructed) were written for this skill and are not his.

## 1. Questions (the steering wheel)

- Present problem: "What's really going on?" "What lets you know that?"
- Clarifying. The invasive word is "why"; use "what": "What makes that important to you?" "When you said X, what specifically did you mean?"
- Trigger moment: "Has this been a stack of things, or was there a recent moment when you realized it needed handling now?" Memories carry emotion; the moment they decided is where the motivation lives.
- Consequence: "If nothing changes over the next year, what happens?" To surface a truth they already hold, never as a threat.
- Future: "If twelve months from now this had gone well, what would have to be true?"
- Gap: "What's your goal, and how close are you?" "What do you think is slowing you down?"
- Transition summary: "So you want X, right now you're at Y, and that's leaving you feeling Z. Is that right?"
- Permission framed: "Is it okay if I challenge you on that?" Permission softens and raises compliance in every domain.
- Criteria: "Isn't X more important than Y?" only when they already said X matters.
- Labelling the emotion: "It sounds like that's been exhausting." Then silence. They go deeper.
- Mirroring: repeat the key two or three words back with a rising tone. "Struggling to get quality leads?"
- Re-ask protocol: a vague answer gets the same question from a different angle. Never accept "it just felt off."
- "Think" for analytical people, "feel" for emotional people. "Do you think this would help?" versus "Do you feel like this would help?"
- Why not lower: when they rate interest or commitment 7 or 8 out of 10, ask "Why not a 4?" They argue themselves upward. Never ask "why not a 10"; that makes them list the reasons against.
- The isolation question, the most valuable line in the system. Sales: "Just so I can be clear on my end, the price point aside, do you feel like this can help you get the results that you want?" General: "Setting [the stated obstacle] aside, do you believe this would work?" It separates the belief from the constraint so you know whether you have a value problem or a structure problem.

## 2. The reframe engine (Sleight of Mouth, 14 patterns)

An objection is an internal conflict: part of them wants it and part cannot justify it. Resolve, do not argue. Stack two to four and stop the moment the tension breaks; running the rest after they have moved creates a new objection.

1. Reality strategy: "How do you know that's true? What specifically?"
2. Model of the world: "Here's how the people who have solved this see it."
3. Counter example: when A did not lead to B. "How many people do you see hustling with nothing to show for it?" "Has there ever been a time when [A] didn't mean [B]?"
4. Intention: fear to purpose. "Why did you get on this call?" At home: "Why did you want to talk about this tonight?"
5. Redefine, the workhorse: "It's not X, it's Y, and here's what that means." Eli's: "It's not that you can't do this. It's that you haven't been trained in it yet. Those are two different problems, and only one of them is permanent."
6. Consequence: "What if nothing changes?" In Mode B, aimed at the situation only.
7. Hierarchy of criteria: "Isn't [what they said matters] more important than [the objection]?"
8. Apply to self: "You sound certain about that belief."
9. Change frame size: zoom out three years, or ten. "Put today's number next to that. Bigger, or smaller?"
10. Meta frame: the belief behind the belief, usually fear or self-trust.
11. Chunk up: to the bigger purpose when they are lost in detail.
12. Chunk down: "How specifically?" "What part, exactly?" when the objection is vague.
13. Metaphor: see the library below.
14. Another outcome: "The real question isn't X, it's Y."

Two more from the practitioner cards: the context reframe ("I'm slow to change" becomes "once you master this, it holds") and the content reframe ("a huge tax bill means you made real money"). Both only when the flip is true.

The self-trust stack: counter example, then redefine, then model of the world, then intention. "I'm broken" becomes "I'm untrained" becomes "aligned with the people who did it" becomes "anchored to why you came."

## 3. Focus-control frames

- Best interest: link every ask to their goal with "so that" or "which means you get to". "I'm going to ask you to push outside your comfort zone so that you get what you're actually capable of." Eli's advanced form: "If I let you stay stuck, I'd be failing you."
- Pre-frame: predict the objection, validate it, reframe it, all before it is raised. Naming their fear first defangs it. Sales: who else is in the decision and what waiting costs, asked in the first ten minutes. Relationship: "I know this is going to sound like criticism at first. It isn't. Can I say it anyway?" (constructed)
- Contrast: never let them compare against zero. Compare against the cost of inaction, a worse alternative, or a bigger price they are already paying.
- Perception of value: people decide by comparison, so lay out the real alternatives with their true costs (doing nothing, doing it alone, the cheaper version). In a negotiation this is the walk-away conversation. At home it is "what does staying like this cost us."
- Anchoring: the first number sets the scale. Biggest true number before the price. Never a fabricated anchor.
- As-if: future-tense language that assumes the good outcome. "When we've handled this, you'll notice..." "If for some reason you did move forward, what would have allowed you to?"
- Probability: "What's the chance this fixes itself, versus with a plan? Which bet do you want?"
- Double commitment: two paths, both forward. "Talk tonight, or tomorrow morning?" In Mode B only when both options are genuinely on offer.
- Disassociation: talk about "the mind", never "you". "The mind is wired to protect us from change, even change we want." Normalize it, show how it has cost them before, hand control back. The safest frame in the system and the one that transfers cleanest to relationships and to self-talk.
- That's actually a good thing: acknowledge, flip (nerves mean it matters, tight money means you'll commit, new means more attention, small means more depth, hard is where the value is), link to people who felt the same, tie to the outcome. Only when the flip is true.
- Aligned adversary: me, then you, then us, then them. The adversary is a belief, a system, an outdated method, a pattern, never the person in front of you.
- Crossroads and two futures: confront the present honestly, one decision, picture both futures. "Which one are you?"
- Three postures: mentor (authority: insight, the proposal), peer (connection: shared struggle), servant (humility: openings, gratitude, the ask). Too superior repels, too equal gets friend-zoned, too inferior loses respect. Business: mostly mentor. Personal: mostly peer, and the mentor posture is where partners get hurt.
- Open loops: unresolved questions hold attention. Open early, delay without frustration, close into the point. The loop you open is the loop you close.
- Magnetic open: the first three to five minutes decide attention. Momentum, agreement amplifiers ("as you already know"), early participation, universality, novelty, pre-framing value. Never drop gold unless they know it's gold.
- Engagement and small commitments: small yeses, participation, breadcrumbs toward the ask. As a way of checking you are still together, never as a trap.
- Urgency and exclusivity: only with real constraints (a true deadline, true capacity, a true cost of waiting). Fabricated scarcity is banned.

## 4. The objection protocol: Isolate, Reframe, Close

Four locks in sales: finances, spouse, timing, fear. Eli's law: "Money, spouse, and timing are never the objection. They are what an internal belief sounds like out loud. Never argue the fact. Change what it means." Fear is the fourth and you see it before you hear it: energy drops, pace changes, answers shorten, agreement without commitment.

1. Acknowledge, take the pressure off. "Yeah, I know it is an investment, I hear you." "We want you to be comfortable more than anything."
2. Isolate. The isolation question. For stalls, the OR question: "Do you feel it's just nerves, OR what's showing up for you?" Two doors, both open, no third door where they stay vague.
3. Deepen on the first real yes. "What are some of the highlights that make you say that it could work?" Their own reasons outweigh anything you could say. Most people walk straight past this sentence.
4. Pivot IF to HOW. "So it's not an IF you think this could work for you, it's more of a HOW can we make this work."
5. Close with structure, only after the isolation question is answered, and never on a fear objection. "If we broke it up so it's just a fraction of the investment up front, would that make it easier?" Structure on fear reads as pressure.
6. Silence. After the price, after a resolved objection, after the close question. Whoever speaks first into the gap gives something away.
7. A new objection is not a failure. A layer came off. Isolate and loop.

Diagnostics that run before any of it:
- Real or smokescreen: "If [the constraint] disappeared, would you move forward?" Hesitation means the real issue is fear or value.
- Fear or logistics: fear gets reframes, logistics get problem-solving. Mismatch destroys trust.
- "Think about it": "What specifically do you want to think over: fit, value, or timing?"
- "Cheaper elsewhere": "If the prices were equal, which would you pick? Why?"
- Third party ("let me talk to my wife"): real or polite exit. Real means help them have the conversation: "What will you tell her if she doesn't understand?" "What resistance might she have?" If the answer is price, you were never in spouse. Never ask them to skip the conversation.

General form of the four locks: resources (money, time), a third party (spouse, board, business partner, a friend's opinion), timing (the stall), and fear (self-trust). Every domain has all four.

## 5. The money moments (where any conversation turns)

1. The pre-frame, early, before any objection exists. Missed it if a partner you never knew about appears at the close.
2. The isolation question, the instant the first objection lands. Missed it if you handled the objection well and a second one appeared immediately.
3. The silence after the number. Missed it if you started justifying a price nobody had challenged.
4. The first real yes. Missed it if you moved to price the second they agreed.
5. The silence after the ask. Missed it if you filled it and the call ended in "let me think about it."

Prevent remorse with two questions before they leave: "What are you most excited about?" and "What made this a now decision for you?"

## 6. Metaphor library

Build new ones by these rules: mirror their situation in a different domain, let them connect the dots, deliver with calm certainty, aim for "I never thought of it that way." Two or three owned metaphors beat a library.

Eli's:
- Broken bone (hesitation): you don't think about a broken arm, you've just gotten used to the pain.
- Leaky roof (cost of waiting): the drip doesn't stay a drip; cheap now is expensive later.
- Frozen lake (doubt): don't blaze a trail across the ice, walk the proven footsteps.
- Parachute (cheap alternatives): cheapest parachute, or the one you trust?
- The island (standing still): the island is familiar, and the tide has been coming in the whole time; you are not choosing between risk and safety, you are choosing which risk you are already taking.

Constructed for this skill, not Eli's; use when they fit:
- Map (doing it alone): you can drive cross-country without a map; what is your time worth?
- Garden (impatience): digging up the seed to check the roots kills the plant.
- Fire alarm (conflict timing): ignoring the smoke alarm doesn't put out the fire, it guarantees a bigger one.
- Two paddlers (drift in a relationship): if only one of us paddles we go in circles; I don't need you to paddle harder, I need to know you're in the boat.
- Riding a bike (learning by doing): a thousand videos and you still can't ride until you get on.

## 7. Delivery notes

- Words are logical influence, tone is emotional influence. Conviction corrects tonality: certainty in the speaker outweighs doubt in the listener.
- Three tonalities: curious, sceptical, empathetic. Pick one per line and say which.
- Short sentences carry the turns (a pivot, a reveal, an ask). Longer sentences carry the teaching.
- Negative first, positive last: "It's going to be hard work, and you'll make more than you ever have. You okay with that?"
- Interrupting about yourself is rude. Interrupting about them ("wait, how hard has that been?") is not.
- Text has no tone, no silence, no pacing. Keep it short, one question, and don't send the second message.
- Mark [silence] in every script where it belongs. Whoever speaks first into it gives something away.
